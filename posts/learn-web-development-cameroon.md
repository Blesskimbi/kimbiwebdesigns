# How to Learn Web Development in Cameroon in 2026

Buea has more people learning to code than anywhere else in the country. The university keeps a steady supply of them, the tech community around Silicon Mountain gives them somewhere to be, and every year a lot of them start and a smaller number finish.

The ones who finish are rarely the most talented. They are the ones who picked a narrow path, built things that other people could see, and kept going for longer than felt reasonable.

This is the route I would take if I were starting again in Cameroon today, including the parts that are specific to being here rather than somewhere with cheap electricity and reliable fibre.

## Key takeaways

- Learn one path properly rather than sampling several. Front end first is the shortest route to work you can be paid for.
- You need about six to twelve months of consistent effort before you are useful to a paying client, not the six weeks a bootcamp advert promises.
- Projects other people can open beat certificates nobody asks about.
- Data costs and power cuts are real constraints here, so download what you can and work offline deliberately.
- The first paid work almost always comes from someone who already knows you.

## Pick one path and stay on it

The most common way to waste a year is to keep starting. A week of Python, then a JavaScript tutorial, then something about machine learning because a video made it sound urgent, then back to the beginning.

For most people here, front end web development is the right first path. It has the shortest distance between learning something and having a thing you can show, it is the easiest to get paid for locally, and it does not require a fast machine.

The order that works:

1. **HTML and CSS.** Structure and layout. [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn) is the reference worth learning to read early. Do not skip this to get to JavaScript faster; almost every ugly site is an ugly site because this step was rushed.
2. **JavaScript.** The language itself. Loops, functions, working with the page, fetching data.
3. **One framework.** React is the safest choice locally, because it is what most job adverts and most freelance clients ask for, and [the official tutorial](https://react.dev/learn) is genuinely good.
4. **Git and GitHub.** How to save your work and show it. This is a small skill that makes you look considerably more professional.
5. **One backend thing.** Enough to store data and handle a login. Supabase or Firebase is plenty to start.

That is a year of steady effort for most people. It is not a weekend.

## Be realistic about how long it takes

The adverts say six weeks. Six weeks gets you a person who can follow a tutorial. It does not get you a person who can be handed a client and left alone.

A more honest timeline, at roughly ten to fifteen hours a week:

| Stage | Time | What you can do at the end |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HTML and CSS | 1 to 2 months | Build a static page that looks like the design |
| JavaScript | 2 to 3 months | Make a page do something, work with data |
| React | 2 to 3 months | Build a multi page app that talks to an API |
| First real project | 1 to 2 months | Something with real users, however few |
| Employable or freelanceable | 6 to 12 months total | Take a small paid job without supervision |

The people who get there faster are usually the ones doing more hours, not the ones who found a shortcut.

## Work around the constraints instead of pretending they do not exist

Two things here shape how you should study, and no international tutorial will mention either.

**Data is not free.** Streaming video lessons all day is expensive. Download courses when you are on wifi and watch them offline. [freeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/) is free, thorough, and works well that way. Read documentation, which is a fraction of the weight of video and usually better. Clone the libraries you need while you have a good connection rather than at the moment you need them.

**Power is not guaranteed.** Keep a charged power bank, and get in the habit of saving and committing your work often, so an unexpected cut costs you five minutes rather than an evening. Learn your editor well enough to work without looking things up online constantly.

Neither of these is a reason not to learn. They are reasons to structure your learning so an interruption is an inconvenience rather than a stop.

## Build things people can open

Nobody has ever asked me for a certificate. Several people have asked me for a link.

Three or four real projects, live on the internet, with the code on GitHub, will do more for you than any qualification you can buy. Not tutorial clones that everyone else also built. Things with a reason to exist.

Ideas that work well here because they solve a visible problem:

- A site for a business you know that does not have one. Offer it free, then use it as a portfolio piece.
- A tool that solves something specific in your own life or your family business.
- Something local that nobody has built. A directory, a booking page for a small service, a price comparison for something people ask about.

Make sure each one is deployed somewhere and loads quickly on a phone. A project that only runs on your laptop cannot be shown to anyone, and a slow one leaves the wrong impression before anyone reads the code.

## The first paid job comes from people, not applications

Almost nobody in Cameroon gets their first paid work by applying cold to a job board. It comes through someone who already knows what you can do.

That means being visible in a way that is not self promotion. Answer questions in developer groups. Show what you are building while you are building it, including when it is unfinished. Go to meetups in Buea when there are meetups in Buea. Help someone with a bug for nothing.

That is also why I run a [free developer community](/community/) on WhatsApp. It is open to complete beginners as well as people already working, it costs nothing, there is no minimum commitment, and the questions people ask there are the same ones I had. Learning next to other people is considerably less discouraging than learning alone.

## Conclusion

Choose one path, give it a year rather than six weeks, and build things that other people can open on their phones. Work around the data and power constraints deliberately instead of being defeated by them. Then get known by a handful of people who can vouch for what you can do.

The market here is not saturated with good developers. It is saturated with people who started and stopped. Finishing is most of the advantage.
